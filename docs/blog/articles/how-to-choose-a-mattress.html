Choosing a mattress is one of the highest-stakes purchases in the home - and one of the least transparent. Marketing language piles up (“orthopaedic”, “cooling”, “hotel luxury”) while the decision that matters is simpler: does this bed support how you sleep, where you live, and how long you plan to keep it?

This guide walks through the factors sleep researchers and mattress engineers consistently point to - without medical claims or miracle numbers. Use it as a checklist before you reserve.

1. Start with how you sleep, not the brochure

Sleep position changes where your body needs pressure relief versus support:

  • Side sleepers usually need more cushioning at the shoulder and hip, with enough midsection support so the spine does not sag into a hammock shape.
  • Back sleepers typically do well with medium to medium-firm feel: the lumbar area should be held up without the hips sinking too far.
  • Stomach sleepers often prefer a firmer surface so the midsection does not drop and force the lower back into extension.
  • Combination sleepers benefit from a balanced hybrid feel that lets you turn without “stuck” sink.

If you share a bed, prioritise the partner who is more sensitive to pressure points - and consider whether motion transfer matters for light sleepers.

2. Firmness is personal - but it is not random

Firmness is usually rated on a soft-medium-firm scale. Body weight and preferred sleep position shift what “medium” feels like. A useful rule of thumb from sleep-product research and retailer fitting practice:

  • Lighter bodies often experience the same mattress as firmer.
  • Heavier bodies often need more deep support so soft foams do not bottom out.

Do not chase the firmest mattress because it sounds “better for your back”. Unsupported hardness can increase pressure points. The goal is aligned comfort - your preferred positions feel stable through the night.

3. Materials: what each layer is for

Most quality beds are systems, not single blocks:

  • Comfort layers (foam, latex, fibre) manage pressure and initial feel.
  • Transition layers stop you from feeling an abrupt drop onto the support core.
  • Support cores (pocket springs or high-density foam) carry weight and hold shape over years.
  • Covers affect breathability, hand-feel and temperature regulation at the skin.

Hybrid constructions (springs + foam) are popular because they combine contouring with airflow through the spring unit. Foam-only beds can feel plush and quiet; spring systems often feel more buoyant and easier to turn on.

4. Size: always check centimetres

Size names are not global. A “King” in the UAE is not the same width as a UK King. Always verify dimensions in centimetres against your bed frame and room.

If you move markets or buy online cross-border, treat the cm figure as the source of truth - not the label.

5. Climate and cool sleep

Heat retention is one of the most common reasons people abandon a mattress. Breathable covers, open coil units and thoughtfully specified foams can help heat move away from the body. Be wary of precise “degrees cooler” claims unless backed by published lab methodology - look instead for construction that is designed for airflow and moisture management.

6. Longevity: replace the worn part, not the whole bed if you can

Comfort layers soften first. Support cores last longer when properly specified. A mattress designed so the comfort top can be refreshed can extend the useful life of the bed and reduce full replacement waste - useful if you want premium comfort without a full rip-and-replace cycle every few years.

7. How to evaluate during a trial

  • Sleep on it for consecutive nights in your normal positions - not a five-minute showroom lie-down alone.
  • Note shoulder/hip pressure, lumbar support, heat build-up, and how easy it is to change position.
  • Use a mattress protector; most trial policies require it.
  • Keep notes for the first fortnight - adaptation is normal when feel changes.
Editorial note: This article is general product guidance, not medical advice. If you have diagnosed spine or sleep conditions, speak with a qualified clinician about mattress needs.

FAQ

How long should a mattress last?

Many households replace every 7-10 years, but construction quality and body weight change that window. Softened comfort layers are often the reason a bed “feels done” before the core fails.

Is a firmer mattress better for back comfort?

Not automatically. Medium support that keeps the spine roughly level is usually more helpful than maximum hardness. Comfort is individual.

What should I decide first: size or firmness?

Confirm size in cm for your frame, then firmness and materials for your sleep position and climate.
